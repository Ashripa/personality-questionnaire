# House-Tree-Person (HTP) Drawing Test

**Priority:** P5
**Category:** Projective personality assessment
**Status:** Published (clinical use)

---

## Overview

The House-Tree-Person (HTP) test is a projective drawing assessment in which individuals are asked to draw a house, a tree, and a person. It is interpreted qualitatively to gain insight into personality, emotional functioning, self-perception, and interpersonal relationships. The closely related Draw-A-Person (DAP) test uses similar principles but focuses on the human figure drawing.

## Structure

| Feature | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| Items | 3 drawings (house, tree, person) + post-drawing inquiry |
| Format | Free drawing on blank paper; followed by structured questions |
| Administration time | 30-90 minutes (including inquiry) |
| Age range | 3 years and older |
| Scoring | Both quantitative (objective) and qualitative (interpretive) |

## Administration

### Phase 1: Drawing (Non-verbal, Unstructured)
1. Subject is given a blank sheet of paper and pencil
2. Asked to draw a house (as good a house as you can)
3. Given a new sheet; asked to draw a tree
4. Given a new sheet; asked to draw a whole person (not a stick figure)
5. Some versions include a second person of the opposite sex

### Phase 2: Post-Drawing Inquiry (Verbal, Structured)
- Standardized questions about each drawing
- For house: "Is it your house? Who lives there? Is it a happy house?"
- For tree: "What kind of tree? How old? Is it alive?"
- For person: "Who is it? How old? What is the person doing/thinking/feeling?"

## Interpretive Framework

### House
- Represents home life, family relationships, and domestic situation
- Roof = intellectual/fantasy life; walls = ego strength; door = openness to environment; windows = contact with environment
- Absence of door or windows may suggest withdrawal

### Tree
- Represents deeper, unconscious self-image and emotional life
- Trunk = ego strength and inner resources; branches = reaching out to the environment; roots = grounding and stability
- Scars or broken branches may indicate psychological trauma

### Person
- Represents conscious self-image and interpersonal relationships
- Size, placement, detail, and proportions are interpreted
- Facial features, hands, and body proportions receive particular attention
- Omissions (missing hands, facial features) are considered significant

## Scoring

### Quantitative Scoring (Buck's System)
- Objective scoring based on presence/absence of specific drawing elements
- Generates raw scores convertible to IQ estimates (original purpose)
- Standardized scoring criteria for each drawing element

### Qualitative Scoring (Clinical Interpretation)
- Projective interpretation of drawing characteristics
- Considers size, placement, pressure, detail, omissions, distortions, erasures
- Examines symbolic content and emotional tone
- Heavily dependent on clinician's training and theoretical orientation

## Psychometric Properties

### Reliability
- Inter-rater reliability: Variable; moderate for quantitative scoring, low for qualitative interpretation
- Test-retest reliability: Generally low to moderate

### Validity
- **Limited empirical evidence for reliability or validity of qualitative/projective interpretations**
- Quantitative scoring has somewhat better psychometric support
- Face validity: High among clinicians
- Criterion validity: Weak and inconsistent across studies
- Incremental validity beyond interviews: Disputed

### Critical Limitations
- Projective hypothesis (drawings reveal unconscious material) is scientifically contested
- Low inter-rater reliability for qualitative interpretation
- Cultural and socioeconomic factors influence drawing ability and content
- Limited evidence that specific drawing features reliably indicate specific personality traits or psychopathology
- Drawing skill confounds interpretation
- May reflect artistic ability more than personality

## Variants

| Variant | Developer | Key Difference |
|---------|-----------|---------------|
| Draw-A-Person (DAP) | Machover (1949) | Person drawing only; personality interpretation |
| Goodenough-Harris Drawing Test | Goodenough (1926), Harris (1963) | Person drawing scored for cognitive development |
| Kinetic Family Drawing (KFD) | Burns & Kaufman (1970) | Family doing something together |
| Kinetic House-Tree-Person (KHTP) | Burns (1987) | All three elements in one drawing with action |
| Chromatic HTP | Buck (1948) | Second set of drawings in color (crayons) |

## Access

- **Published:** Western Psychological Services (WPS)
- **Manual:** Buck, J. N. (revised by Warren, W. L.) available from WPS
- **Cost:** Moderate (manual and scoring forms)
- **No item copyright concerns:** The "items" are simply instructions to draw
- **Training:** Requires clinical training in projective assessment for interpretation
- **Qualification level:** Typically Level B or C (requires graduate-level clinical training)

## Notes

- Despite psychometric limitations, HTP remains widely used in clinical practice, especially in child psychology, forensic evaluations, and cross-cultural settings
- Its non-verbal nature makes it useful with children, individuals with language barriers, or those resistant to verbal assessment
- Most professional guidelines recommend using HTP as a supplementary tool alongside standardized instruments, not as a standalone diagnostic measure
- The American Psychological Association and major assessment textbooks note the weak empirical support for projective drawing interpretations
