# Amae (Japanese) and Jeong (Korean) Cultural Personality Concepts

**Priority:** P5
**Category:** Culture-specific personality / relational concepts
**Status:** Theoretical constructs with associated research measures

---

## Overview

Amae and Jeong are culture-specific psychological concepts from Japan and Korea, respectively, that describe distinctive patterns of interpersonal relatedness, emotional dependence, and affective bonding. Neither is a standardized psychometric instrument in the Western sense; rather, they represent indigenous psychological constructs that have been operationalized in various research measures and that challenge Western assumptions about personality as individual-centered.

---

## Part I: Amae (Japanese)

### Key Citation
- Doi, T. (1971). *Amae no Kozo* [The Anatomy of Dependence]. Tokyo: Kobundo. (English translation: 1973, Kodansha International.)
- Doi, T. (1986). *The Anatomy of Self: The Individual Versus Society*. Kodansha International.
- Yamaguchi, S. (2004). Further clarifications of the concept of amae in relation to dependence and attachment. *Human Development, 47*(1), 28-33.

### Core Concept

**Amae** refers to the desire to be passively loved, indulged, and cared for by another person -- a form of pleasurable, presumptive dependence. The prototype is the infant's relationship with the mother, but in Japanese culture, amae extends into adult relationships, workplace dynamics, and social interactions.

### Key Features
- **Presumptive dependence:** Expecting to be indulged without having to ask explicitly
- **Passive love:** Desire to be unconditionally accepted and cared for
- **Non-verbal communication:** Amae often operates through implicit understanding rather than verbal requests
- **Bidirectional:** Both the person seeking amae and the person providing it participate in a culturally valued exchange
- **Not pathological:** Unlike Western "dependency" (which carries negative connotations), amae is considered a natural, healthy aspect of human relationships in Japanese culture

### Doi's Theoretical Contributions
- Argued that amae is central to understanding the Japanese psyche
- Proposed that Western psychology's emphasis on independence and autonomy reflects cultural bias
- Distinguished amae from clinical dependency: amae involves trust and intimacy, not insecurity
- Identified frustration of amae needs as a source of psychopathology in Japanese culture

### Research Measures
- No single standardized scale dominates the field
- Researchers have developed ad hoc measures for specific studies
- Yamaguchi and colleagues developed amae scales distinguishing between "appropriate amae" and "inappropriate amae"
- Some studies use vignette-based measures or behavioral observation

## Part II: Jeong / Cheong (Korean)

### Key Citation
- Choi, S. C., Kim, U., & Choi, S. H. (1993). Indigenous analysis of collective representations: A Korean perspective. In U. Kim & J. W. Berry (Eds.), *Indigenous Psychologies: Research and Experience in Cultural Context* (pp. 193-210). Sage.
- Choi, S. C., & Choi, S. H. (2001). Cheong: The socioemotional grammar of Koreans. *International Journal of Group Tensions, 30*(1), 69-80.
- Choi, S. C. (2000). *Korean Psychology*. Chungang University Press. (In Korean.)

### Core Concept

**Jeong** (also romanized as cheong or jung) is a Korean concept describing deep emotional bonds, attachment, and affection that develop over time through shared experiences and proximity. It encompasses feelings of warmth, compassion, sympathy, and a sense of inseparable connectedness that accumulates in relationships.

### Key Features
- **Cumulative:** Jeong builds over time through shared experiences (unlike instant attraction)
- **Involuntary:** Once formed, jeong persists even when the relationship becomes difficult or the person is disliked ("miun jeong" -- hateful jeong)
- **Extends beyond humans:** Koreans describe jeong toward places, objects, pets, and even habits
- **Not strictly positive:** Jeong includes bittersweet and ambivalent feelings; it can create burdensome obligations
- **Social glue:** Functions as the affective infrastructure of Korean social life

### Types of Jeong
| Type | Description |
|------|-------------|
| **Goeun jeong** | Beautiful/positive jeong; warm affection |
| **Miun jeong** | Hateful jeong; attachment despite negative feelings |
| **Jeongi deulda** | Jeong develops (becoming attached) |
| **Jeongi tteoleojida** | Jeong falls away (losing attachment) |
| **Jeongi meokda** | Eating jeong (deeply invested) |

### Research Measures
- Choi and colleagues developed scales measuring dimensions of jeong
- Measures typically assess: depth of feeling, sense of inseparability, willingness to sacrifice, nostalgia
- Research primarily conducted in Korean; limited English-language instruments

## Comparison: Amae vs. Jeong

| Feature | Amae (Japanese) | Jeong (Korean) |
|---------|----------------|----------------|
| Core quality | Presumptive dependence, desire to be indulged | Deep accumulated emotional bond |
| Temporal nature | Can be momentary or sustained | Builds over time; cumulative |
| Directionality | Often asymmetric (receiver-provider) | Often mutual and symmetric |
| Prototypical relationship | Mother-child | Long-term companions, community members |
| Valence | Generally positive | Mixed (includes miun jeong) |
| Cultural function | Facilitates intimacy and trust | Provides social cohesion and belonging |
| Closest Western concepts | Dependency, attachment (but not equivalent) | Bonding, rapport, nostalgia (but not equivalent) |

## Psychometric Considerations

### Challenges in Measurement
- Both concepts resist reduction to Likert-scale items due to their contextual, relational nature
- Existing measures have limited validation outside their culture of origin
- Translation inevitably alters meaning, as the concepts are deeply embedded in cultural practices and language
- Social desirability may inflate endorsement of culturally valued experiences

### Cross-Cultural Relevance
- Both concepts challenge the Western assumption that independence and autonomy are universally adaptive
- They suggest that personality assessment must account for relational and interdependent self-construals
- Growing interest in incorporating these concepts into cross-cultural personality frameworks
- Related to Markus and Kitayama's (1991) distinction between independent and interdependent self-construals

## Significance for Personality Assessment

1. **Challenge to universalism:** Demonstrates that key personality dimensions in one culture may have no direct equivalent in others
2. **Relational personality:** Suggests that personality may be better understood as patterns of interpersonal relating rather than individual traits
3. **Indigenous psychology movement:** Both concepts are exemplars of the argument that psychology must develop constructs from within cultures, not just import Western frameworks
4. **Assessment implications:** Standard personality inventories (Big Five, MBTI) may systematically miss dimensions like amae and jeong that are central to personality in East Asian cultures

## Access

- **Doi's works:** Available in English translation (widely accessible in libraries)
- **Choi's works:** Primarily in Korean; key chapters available in English edited volumes
- **Research measures:** Published in journal articles; no single standardized commercial instrument for either concept
- **No commercial assessment products** for either concept

## Notes

- Amae and Jeong are not directly equivalent, despite superficial similarities; they reflect different cultural logics of relatedness
- Both concepts are actively researched in their respective countries and in cross-cultural psychology
- They represent important cases for the argument that personality psychology cannot be culture-free
- Understanding these concepts is essential for clinicians, coaches, and researchers working with Japanese and Korean populations
